Methodology

How scores, percentiles and classifications are worked out, and where the limits are.

Last updated October 1, 2026

The IQ scale we use

Thinkora reports IQ on the standard deviation IQ scale. Scores are set so that the average is 100 and the standard deviation is 15, which is the scale used by the Wechsler tests and most modern IQ tests. About 68% of people fall between 85 and 115, and about 95% fall between 70 and 130.

How we turn a score into a percentile

We assume scores follow a normal distribution. A score is converted to a z-score, which is how many standard deviations it sits from 100:

z = (IQ − 100) ÷ SD

The percentile is the area under the standard normal curve to the left of that z-score. For a score of 130 on the SD 15 scale, z is 2 and the percentile is about 97.7. The “1 in N” figures use the area in the tail beyond the same z-score.

The percentile calculator also supports SD 16 and SD 24, because some older and culture-fair tests use those scales. Changing the SD changes the z-score for the same number, so the same IQ can mean a different rank depending on the test.

Classification bands

We use the familiar ranges from the Wechsler scales: 130 and above is very superior, 120 to 129 superior, 110 to 119 high average, 90 to 109 average, 80 to 89 low average, 70 to 79 borderline, and below 70 extremely low. These are descriptive labels for a position on the curve. They are not diagnoses. See the IQ score guide for the share of people in each band.

The Thinkora IQ test

The Thinkora IQ test gives you an estimated IQ. Your number of correct answers is converted to the standard scale, and the percentile is then worked out with the method above. Because we do not norm the test against a large, stratified sample the way a clinical test is, the result carries a wider margin of error than a professional assessment.

Brain games

Games such as the reaction time test report results in their own units, for example milliseconds or the number of digits remembered. These are not converted into IQ points. They show how you perform on one narrow task, and they are best used to compare yourself with your own earlier results. Screen refresh rate, input device and browser all add a small delay, as explained in our reaction time article .

Limits you should know about

  • Online tests cannot control the test environment, the device or the person taking the test.
  • Percentiles assume a perfect bell curve. Real score distributions are close to it in the middle but less certain at the extremes.
  • Very high and very low scores are the hardest to measure, so treat them with the most caution.
  • No Thinkora result is a clinical assessment or a basis for medical, educational or employment decisions.

Editorial standards and corrections

We write articles for a general reader and base statistics on the normal distribution or on published research. When new evidence changes the picture, we update the article and change its “last updated” date. If you find an error, please contact us and we will check it.
