IQ Test Methodology

How Thinkora calculates estimated IQ scores, percentiles and classifications, and what the results can and cannot tell you.

Last updated October 1, 2026

How Thinkora measures IQ

Thinkora provides an estimated IQ score based on performance on our online IQ test. The test uses questions designed around different types of reasoning, including spatial, numerical, logical, verbal and applied reasoning.

The result is reported on the familiar IQ scale, where the average is 100 and the standard deviation is 15. This makes it possible to express a score as a position relative to a reference distribution.

The standard IQ scale

Thinkora uses a standard deviation of 15 when displaying its estimated IQ results. On this scale, an IQ of 100 represents the mean. About 68% of scores in a normal distribution fall between 85 and 115, while about 95% fall between 70 and 130.

These figures describe the mathematical properties of the standard normal model. They do not mean that every real-world population has exactly the same distribution of IQ scores.

How an IQ score is converted to a percentile

An IQ score can be converted into a percentile by comparing it with a normal distribution. The first step is calculating a z-score, which describes how many standard deviations a score is above or below the mean:

z = (IQ − 100) ÷ SD

For the standard SD 15 scale, an IQ of 130 gives a z-score of 2. The area to the left of z = 2 is approximately 97.7%, so an IQ of 130 corresponds to about the 97.7th percentile under this model.

The IQ percentile calculator can also calculate percentiles using SD 16 and SD 24. Because changing the standard deviation changes the z-score, the same numerical IQ score can correspond to a different percentile depending on the scale being used.

How Thinkora estimates your IQ

When you complete the Thinkora IQ test, your performance is used to produce an estimated score on the standard IQ scale. The corresponding percentile is then calculated using the normal-distribution method described above.

Thinkora does not claim that this online score has the same precision as a professionally administered standardized assessment. Our test has not been normed against a large, representative population sample in the same way as established clinical intelligence tests.

IQ classification ranges

Thinkora's IQ score guide uses familiar descriptive ranges such as average, high average, superior and very superior. These labels describe a person's position on an IQ scale; they are not medical diagnoses and should not be interpreted as a complete description of a person.

How the brain games are scored

Thinkora's brain games are separate from the IQ scoring system. They measure performance on specific tasks rather than converting game results into IQ points.

For example, the reaction time test reports milliseconds, while the number memory game measures how many digits you can remember. These results should not be treated as IQ scores.

Device performance, screen refresh rate, browser behavior and input method can affect some browser-based measurements, particularly reaction-time results. See our reaction time results guide for more information.

Important limitations

  • Online tests cannot fully control the environment, device or testing conditions.
  • A short online test cannot measure every aspect of cognitive ability.
  • Normal-distribution percentiles are mathematical estimates and become less certain at extreme scores.
  • Very high and very low results should therefore be interpreted with particular caution.
  • Thinkora results are not clinical assessments and should not be used as the sole basis for medical, educational, employment or other professional decisions.

Why IQ results can differ between tests

Two IQ tests can produce different scores for the same person. Tests may use different questions, scoring systems, standard deviations, reference populations and testing conditions.

Performance can also vary from one testing session to another. Familiarity with the format, concentration, fatigue, distractions and other testing conditions can affect performance.

For that reason, a single online IQ score should not be treated as an exact or permanent measurement of intelligence.

Editorial standards and corrections

Thinkora aims to explain IQ testing and related topics in straightforward language. Mathematical examples are based on the stated IQ scale and normal-distribution assumptions, while claims about research are intended to be supported by published evidence.

If an error or unclear explanation is identified, we review the information and make corrections where appropriate. If you notice something that needs checking, please contact Thinkora.
