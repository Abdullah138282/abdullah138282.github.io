Reaction time test results explained

Updated October 1, 2026  ·  6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Reaction time is usually measured in milliseconds (ms), with a lower number meaning a faster recorded response.
  • There is no single reaction time score that is "good" for everyone because results depend on the task, person and testing equipment.
  • Screen refresh rate, input device, browser, computer, attention, fatigue and other factors can affect an online reaction time test.
  • Reaction time is a specific performance measure and should not be treated as a direct measure of IQ or intelligence.

What is a good reaction time?

For a simple visual reaction-time test, many people produce results in the low-to-mid hundreds of milliseconds. However, there is no universal cutoff that defines a "good reaction time." Results depend on the person, type of test, device and testing conditions.

Reaction time is normally reported in milliseconds (ms). One millisecond is one thousandth of a second. A lower number means the recorded response happened sooner.

When comparing reaction time test results, it is more useful to compare scores produced under similar conditions than to focus on one isolated number.

What does a reaction time test measure?

A simple visual reaction time test measures how quickly you respond after a visual signal appears. Your eyes detect the change, your brain processes the information, a response is selected and your muscles produce the movement needed to press a button or click.

The recorded result therefore reflects several processes at once, including attention, visual processing, decision-making and motor response. The test equipment also contributes to the final measurement.

What does reaction time in milliseconds mean?

Reaction time scores are easier to understand when you remember that smaller numbers represent faster responses.

  • 200 ms: the recorded response took two tenths of a second.
  • 250 ms: the response took one quarter of a second.
  • 300 ms: the response took three tenths of a second.

These numbers should not be treated as universal performance categories. The same person can obtain different results on different devices or reaction-time tests.

What affects an online reaction time test?

An online reaction-time result measures more than your physical response. The computer or phone used for the test can introduce additional timing differences.

  • Screen refresh rate. Displays update at particular intervals, which can influence when a visual change becomes available to the user.
  • Display processing. Different screens can introduce different amounts of processing delay before an image change becomes visible.
  • Mouse, keyboard or touchscreen. Input devices can have different response characteristics, so the recorded time can vary between devices.
  • Browser and computer activity. Background processes and system activity can affect the consistency of browser-based measurements.
  • Device type. A desktop computer, laptop, tablet and phone may produce different results even when the same person performs the test.

What can change your reaction time?

  • Age. Reaction time can change across the lifespan, and slower responses generally become more common with increasing age.
  • Sleep and fatigue. Being tired or insufficiently rested can reduce alertness and make responses slower or less consistent.
  • Attention. Distractions and reduced concentration can increase response times and create greater variation between attempts.
  • Caffeine. Caffeine can affect alertness and performance, although the response can vary between individuals.
  • Practice. Familiarity with the test can improve performance, especially during early attempts.

Why is my reaction time different every time?

Small differences between reaction time test results are normal. Your attention, anticipation, fatigue, device timing and familiarity with the task can all affect the recorded result.

One unusually fast attempt does not necessarily represent your typical reaction time, just as one unusually slow attempt does not necessarily mean your ability suddenly became worse.

If you want to track changes, use the same device, browser and testing setup and compare several attempts rather than focusing only on your fastest score.

How to get a more consistent reaction time score

  • Use the same device whenever you compare results.
  • Keep your hand and input position consistent.
  • Test in a reasonably quiet environment.
  • Avoid testing when you are extremely tired or distracted.
  • Complete several rounds instead of relying on one attempt.
  • Compare your average or typical performance rather than only your personal fastest result.

Does reaction time measure IQ?

Reaction time and IQ are related to some aspects of cognitive performance, but a simple reaction-time test cannot be used to determine an individual's IQ accurately.

Reaction time measures a narrower aspect of performance than an IQ test. IQ tests can involve several different abilities, including reasoning, numerical thinking, spatial reasoning and other cognitive skills.

A slower reaction-time result does not by itself indicate poor reasoning ability or low intelligence.

Can you improve your reaction time?

Performance on a reaction-time task can improve with practice, particularly when you become familiar with the test and its timing. However, improvement on one specific task should not automatically be interpreted as a permanent change in every aspect of reaction speed.

If your goal is to track your performance, practising under consistent conditions and comparing results over multiple attempts gives you a more useful picture than chasing one unusually fast score.

What is the average reaction time?

There is no single average reaction time that applies to every reaction-time test. Visual, auditory and choice reaction tasks measure different responses, and results also vary with age, equipment and testing conditions.

For simple online visual tests, results in the hundreds of milliseconds are common. Because browser and device timing can influence the measurement, online results should be interpreted as an estimate of performance on that particular task.

Online reaction time vs. laboratory testing

A browser-based reaction-time test is convenient and useful for personal comparisons, but it does not provide the same level of timing control as specialized laboratory equipment.

Laboratory testing can control equipment, stimulus presentation and response recording more precisely. An online test is better suited to casual measurement, practice and tracking your own performance under consistent conditions.

How to interpret your result

Treat your reaction-time score as a measurement of your performance on a particular task at a particular moment. Compare results using the same setup, look at several attempts, and avoid treating a single number as a complete measure of intelligence or ability.

What should you take away?

A reaction time test measures how quickly you respond to a particular stimulus. A lower result in milliseconds represents a faster recorded response, but there is no universal score that is "good" for every person and every test.

Your result can be influenced by attention, fatigue, practice, age and the equipment used. For online testing, screen, browser and input-device differences are especially important when comparing scores.

The most useful approach is to use the same setup, complete multiple attempts and compare your own typical performance over time.
