Reaction time test results explained

Updated October 1, 2026  ·  5 min read

Key takeaways

  • On a simple visual reaction-time task, results in the low-to-mid hundreds of milliseconds are common, but there is no single score that defines a "good" reaction time for everyone.
  • Your screen, input device, browser, computer and network or system conditions can affect an online measurement.
  • Reaction time is a specific performance measure and should not be treated as a direct measure of IQ or overall intelligence.

What is a good reaction time?

For a simple visual reaction-time test, many people produce results somewhere in the low-to-mid hundreds of milliseconds. Results can vary substantially depending on the task, equipment, age, attention and testing conditions, so there is no universal cutoff for a "good" score.

Reaction time is usually reported in milliseconds (ms). One millisecond is one thousandth of a second. A lower number means the response happened sooner, but a very low online result should be interpreted in the context of the device and test being used.

What happens during a reaction-time test?

In a simple visual task, your eyes detect a change, your brain processes the information and selects a response, signals travel to the muscles involved, and your finger or hand produces the response. The recorded time includes the combined delay of these processes as well as the characteristics of the testing device.

Reaction time is therefore not controlled by one single factor. Attention, alertness, motor response and the type of stimulus can all influence the result.

What adds delay to an online test?

  • Screen refresh and display. A display does not update continuously. Its refresh rate and display processing can affect when a visual change becomes available to you.
  • Input device. A mouse, keyboard or touchscreen can introduce different amounts of input delay. The result can therefore differ between devices.
  • Browser and system activity. Heavy system activity, background processes or browser conditions can sometimes affect the consistency of an online measurement.
  • Device differences. Two computers or phones can produce different measurements even when the person responding is the same.

Because an online reaction-time test includes both human response and equipment-related timing, it is most useful for tracking your own performance under similar conditions rather than as a laboratory-grade measurement.

What can change your reaction time?

  • Age. Reaction time generally changes across the lifespan, with slower responses becoming more common as people get older.
  • Sleep and fatigue. Insufficient sleep and tiredness can reduce alertness and slow or make responses less consistent.
  • Attention. Distractions and reduced concentration can increase response times and make results more variable.
  • Caffeine and other stimulants. Caffeine can affect alertness and reaction performance, although the size and direction of an individual's response can vary.
  • Practice. Familiarity with the task can improve performance, particularly during early attempts.

How to get a fair reaction-time result

  • Use the same device and browser when comparing results.
  • Keep your hand and input position consistent between attempts.
  • Take several rounds rather than judging yourself from one unusually fast or slow response.
  • Test when you are reasonably rested and free from distractions.
  • Compare your average or typical results rather than focusing only on your single fastest attempt.

Does reaction time measure IQ?

Research has found relationships between performance on some reaction-time tasks and scores on intelligence tests. However, the relationship is not strong enough to calculate an individual's IQ from a simple reaction-time result.

Reaction time measures a narrower aspect of performance than an IQ test, and the two should not be treated as interchangeable. A slower reaction-time result does not by itself indicate poor reasoning ability or low intelligence.

Why your score can change from one attempt to another

Small differences between attempts are normal. Your attention, anticipation, fatigue, device timing and familiarity with the task can all affect the recorded number.

If your goal is to see whether your performance is changing, use the same setup and compare several attempts across time. This gives you a more useful picture than treating one result as a permanent measure of your ability.
