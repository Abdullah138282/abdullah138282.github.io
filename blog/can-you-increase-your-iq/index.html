Can you increase your IQ?

Updated October 1, 2026  ·  6 min read

Key takeaways

  • IQ scores are relatively stable, especially in adulthood, but they can change over time and are influenced by both individual and environmental factors.
  • Education has some of the strongest evidence for producing lasting gains in cognitive test performance, although the size of the effect varies.
  • Sleep, physical activity, learning and good test conditions can support cognitive performance, but they should not be presented as guaranteed ways to permanently raise IQ.

Can you increase your IQ?

The short answer is: IQ scores can change, but there is no simple method that reliably produces a large, permanent increase for everyone. Cognitive test scores are influenced by age, education, health, environment, familiarity with testing and other factors.

IQ is also not completely fixed across a person's lifetime. Research on changes between generations, including the Flynn effect, shows that average performance on intelligence tests can change substantially under different environmental conditions.

How stable is IQ?

IQ scores tend to become more stable as people get older. In adulthood, an individual's relative position on cognitive tests is generally fairly consistent, although scores can still move because of development, education, health, life circumstances, measurement error and the particular test being taken.

This means that a change in an IQ score does not necessarily mean that a person's underlying cognitive abilities changed by the same amount. Different tests also measure different combinations of reasoning, memory, processing speed, language and other abilities.

Education has some of the strongest evidence

Research on education and intelligence has found that additional schooling is associated with higher performance on cognitive tests, and some research suggests that the relationship is partly causal rather than simply a result of more able people staying in education longer.

The size of the effect varies across studies, ages, educational settings and the type of cognitive test used. Education should therefore not be treated as a guaranteed way to add a specific number of IQ points to every individual.

What about brain-training games?

Brain-training apps and games can make you better at the specific tasks you practise. The more difficult question is whether those improvements transfer to unrelated abilities or produce a broad increase in general intelligence.

Research has generally found stronger evidence for improvement on trained tasks than for large improvements in general cognitive ability. If you enjoy brain games, they can still be useful as practice and entertainment, but a higher game score should not automatically be interpreted as a higher IQ.

Things that can help you perform at your best

  • Sleep. Adequate sleep supports attention, learning and memory. Poor sleep can temporarily make concentration and problem solving more difficult.
  • Physical activity. Regular physical activity is associated with several aspects of physical and cognitive health. It is better viewed as part of overall health than as a guaranteed IQ-boosting method.
  • Learning challenging subjects. Learning a language, instrument, technical subject or other demanding skill develops knowledge and provides sustained cognitive practice.
  • Reading and learning. Reading can build vocabulary and background knowledge, which contribute to performance on some cognitive tasks.
  • Managing test conditions. Fatigue, distractions, anxiety and unfamiliar testing conditions can affect performance. A quiet environment and adequate rest can help you show your current ability more consistently.

Can practising IQ tests raise your score?

Often, taking similar tests repeatedly can produce a practice effect. You may become more familiar with the question formats, timing and strategies used by the test.

That can improve performance on a later test without necessarily representing the same-size change in general cognitive ability. For that reason, repeatedly taking very similar IQ tests is not a reliable way to determine whether your underlying ability has changed.

What is a realistic goal?

Instead of focusing only on a single IQ number, it can be more useful to build skills that matter in everyday life: learning, memory, concentration, reasoning, reading and problem solving. These abilities can be developed in different ways, even when your measured IQ remains relatively stable.

If you want to track particular skills over time, Thinkora also has a reaction time test, number memory test and pattern memory game. Compare your own results over time rather than treating one online score as a permanent measure of ability.

What should you take away?

There is no single proven shortcut for permanently increasing IQ. Education can influence cognitive test performance, while sleep, health, learning and good testing conditions can help you perform closer to your current potential. Practice can also improve your familiarity with a particular test.

Most importantly, an IQ score is only one measure of cognitive performance. If you take an online test, treat the result as an estimate rather than a permanent label. For a deeper explanation of how scores are produced, read how IQ is calculated and see Thinkora's IQ test methodology.
