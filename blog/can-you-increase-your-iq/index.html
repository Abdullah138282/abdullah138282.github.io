Can you increase your IQ?

Updated October 1, 2026  ·  6 min read

Key takeaways

  • IQ is fairly stable in adults, but it is not fixed.
  • Education has the best evidence for raising scores, and brain-training games show limited transfer.
  • Sleep, exercise and practice help you perform at your best, even if they do not raise ability.

Is IQ fixed?

No, but it is fairly stable. Scores in adulthood tend to stay in the same area over time, and people who test high at one age tend to test high at another. Still, scores can move, and genes are only part of the story. Education, health and environment all play a role, as the Flynn effect shows at population level.

What has the best evidence: education

Studies of schooling suggest that staying in education longer is linked with higher test scores. A large review of studies on this question found that each extra year of education was associated with a gain of roughly one to five IQ points. Researchers continue to debate how large the effect is for any one person, but of all the factors studied, schooling has the strongest support.

Brain training games

Apps that promise to make you smarter are popular. Reviews of the research find that people get much better at the games they practise, but that the improvement rarely transfers to different tasks or to general intelligence. If you enjoy them, play for fun, and do not expect your IQ to rise.

Things that help you perform at your best

  • Sleep. A tired brain slows down and makes more mistakes. Regular, sufficient sleep supports attention and memory.
  • Physical activity. Regular exercise is linked with better thinking and brain health, especially as people age.
  • Learning new, hard things. A new language, an instrument or a difficult subject builds knowledge and gives your brain varied practice.
  • Reading widely. Reading grows vocabulary and general knowledge, which feed into many test items.
  • Managing stress. Anxiety takes up working memory. Staying calm helps you use what you have.

Practice and test familiarity

Taking an IQ test again often gives a slightly higher score, because you already know the question types. This is called a practice effect. It shows you got better at the test, and it is not strong evidence that your underlying ability changed. Practising puzzles before a test can help you feel at ease, but it will not turn an average score into an exceptional one.

A realistic goal

Rather than chasing a higher number, focus on skills you can use: memory, focus, reading, problem solving. These improve with practice and help in everyday life whatever your score is. If you want to track your focus or speed over time, try the reaction time test and the number memory test, and compare yourself with your own past results.
