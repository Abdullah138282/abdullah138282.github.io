The Flynn effect explained

Updated October 1, 2026  ·  5 min read

Key takeaways

  • The Flynn effect describes the rise in average performance on many intelligence tests observed across much of the 20th century.
  • IQ tests are periodically re-normed, which keeps the average score close to 100 even when raw performance changes over time.
  • Researchers have proposed several explanations, including changes in education, health, nutrition and the environment, but there is no single explanation that accounts for every finding.

What is the Flynn effect?

The Flynn effect is the finding that average performance on many intelligence tests increased across much of the 20th century in a number of countries. It is named after the New Zealand researcher James Flynn, who helped document and popularize the pattern in the 1980s.

A commonly cited estimate is an increase of roughly three IQ-scale points per decade in many populations, although the size of the effect has varied by country, test, age group and historical period. The pattern therefore should not be treated as one fixed rate that applies everywhere.

Why does the average stay at 100?

IQ tests are periodically re-normed. Test publishers collect performance data from a new reference sample and establish a new scoring system so that the average score on that norm group is set around 100.

This means that raw performance can change across generations without the average reported IQ moving away from 100. A score therefore only has meaning in relation to the norms and scoring system used by the particular test.

Leading explanations

  • Education. Changes in the amount and type of schooling may contribute because education can develop skills involved in abstract, rule-based and problem-solving tasks.
  • Health and nutrition. Improvements in childhood health, nutrition and living conditions may have supported cognitive development.
  • A changing cognitive environment. Modern environments contain more symbols, diagrams, technology and other forms of abstract information that may influence performance on some types of reasoning tasks.
  • Other social and environmental changes. Family size, occupational demands, test familiarity and other changes have also been investigated as possible contributors.

Researchers continue to debate the relative importance of these factors. The evidence does not point to one simple cause that explains the entire effect across every country and period.

Is the effect still going on?

The pattern has not remained the same everywhere. Research in several countries, including some Northern European populations, has reported periods in which scores levelled off or declined after earlier gains. This later pattern is sometimes described as a reverse Flynn effect.

The timing and size of these changes vary between countries, tests and populations, and researchers continue to investigate why the trends differ. Earlier gains have also not been identical across all types of cognitive tasks.

What the Flynn effect does and does not mean

The Flynn effect shows that performance on intelligence tests can change substantially across generations. That is important because it demonstrates that test performance is influenced by environmental and historical conditions rather than representing a completely fixed quantity.

It does not by itself prove that people became more intelligent in every sense. Different types of cognitive tasks have shown different patterns of change, and higher test performance does not automatically translate into a universal increase in every aspect of cognition.

Why old IQ scores need context

Because IQ tests are periodically re-normed, an IQ score from one generation should not automatically be compared directly with a score from another generation without considering the norms used by each test.
