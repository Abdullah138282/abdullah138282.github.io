The Flynn effect explained

Updated October 1, 2026  ·  5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Raw scores on IQ tests rose by about 3 points per decade across much of the 20th century.
  • Tests are re-normed so the average stays at 100, which hides the rise.
  • Better schooling, health and nutrition, and familiarity with abstract thinking are leading explanations.

What is the Flynn effect?

The Flynn effect is the finding that, over much of the 20th century, people in many countries scored higher on IQ tests each decade. It is named after the New Zealand researcher James Flynn, who documented it in the 1980s. The usual estimate is a gain of about three IQ points per decade in many countries, although the size differs by country, test and period.

Put another way, an average person in 1950 who took a test today, scored against modern norms, would land below 100. And an average person today scored against 1950 norms would come out well above 100.

Why does the average stay at 100?

IQ tests are re-normed every so often. Test publishers collect a fresh sample and set the average back to 100. The raw scores have gone up, but the IQ number is reset, so the rise is easy to miss. That is why a score only means something when you know which norms it was measured against.

Leading explanations

  • More and better schooling. Longer time in education trains the kind of abstract, rule-based thinking that IQ tests reward.
  • Health and nutrition. Better early-life nutrition and fewer childhood illnesses support brain development.
  • A more complex world. Modern life is full of symbols, maps, screens and puzzles, which may train pattern-spotting skills.
  • Smaller families and test familiarity. More parental attention per child and more experience with tests may add a little.

Researchers still debate how much each factor counts, and most think several act together.

Is the effect still going on?

Not everywhere. Some studies in Northern Europe, such as in Norway, Denmark and Finland, have reported that scores levelled off or fell in recent decades, sometimes called the reverse Flynn effect. Researchers disagree about the causes and whether the pattern is the same elsewhere. Many developing countries have continued to see gains as schooling and health improve.

What it does and does not mean

The Flynn effect shows that test scores respond to environment, not only to something fixed. It does not prove that people became more “intelligent” in every sense. Gains were larger on abstract reasoning tasks than on vocabulary or arithmetic, which suggests that people got better at certain kinds of thinking.
