The Flynn effect explained

Updated October 1, 2026  ·  5 min read

Key takeaways

  • The Flynn effect describes the rise in average performance on many intelligence tests observed across much of the 20th century.
  • The size of the effect has varied by country, test, age group and historical period.
  • IQ tests are periodically re-normed, which keeps the average score of the new reference group around 100.
  • Researchers have proposed several environmental and social explanations rather than one single cause.
  • In some populations, earlier gains have slowed, stopped or reversed during more recent periods.

What is the Flynn effect?

The Flynn effect is the observed rise in average performance on many intelligence tests across generations. The pattern was documented in numerous countries during much of the 20th century and became widely associated with New Zealand researcher James Flynn, who studied and described the phenomenon in detail.

One commonly cited estimate is an increase of roughly three IQ-scale points per decade in some populations. However, this is not a universal rate. The size of the Flynn effect has differed depending on the country, test, age group, cognitive ability and period being studied.

Why did IQ test scores increase over time?

The exact reason for the Flynn effect is still debated. Researchers have investigated several possible explanations involving changes in education, health, nutrition, family environments and the cognitive demands of modern life.

These explanations are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Changes in several parts of the environment may have contributed to changes in performance on different types of intelligence tests.

Possible causes of the Flynn effect

  • Education. Increased access to schooling and changes in educational practices may have contributed to stronger performance on some abstract reasoning and problem-solving tasks.
  • Health and nutrition. Improvements in childhood health, nutrition and living conditions may support cognitive development and therefore influence test performance.
  • Environmental changes. Modern environments expose people to more symbols, diagrams, technology and abstract information than many earlier environments did.
  • Family and social changes. Changes in family size, home environments and other social conditions have also been examined as possible contributors.
  • Changing cognitive demands. Work, education and everyday activities can place different demands on reasoning and information-processing skills across historical periods.

No single explanation has been established as a complete account of the Flynn effect. Different explanations may apply to different populations, time periods or types of cognitive tests.

Does the Flynn effect mean people became more intelligent?

Not necessarily in every sense of the word intelligence. The Flynn effect concerns changes in performance on particular intelligence tests. Those tests measure selected cognitive abilities, so an increase in test performance does not automatically mean that every aspect of human cognition increased by the same amount.

This distinction matters because different types of cognitive tasks have not always shown identical patterns of change. Test performance is influenced by the interaction between a person's abilities and the environment in which those abilities develop.

Why is the average IQ still 100?

A common point of confusion is that average IQ scores remain centered around 100 even though the Flynn effect describes increases in test performance.

The reason is re-norming. When a test publisher develops a new version or updates its norms, performance data are collected from a new reference sample. The scoring system is then established so that the new norm group has the intended average, typically around 100.

Therefore, a person's raw performance can change across generations while the reported IQ scale continues to have an average of about 100 for the current norm group.

What does re-norming an IQ test mean?

Re-norming means establishing new reference data and scoring rules for a test using a more recent population. This helps keep scores interpretable relative to the population for which the test is currently intended.

Without updated norms, historical changes in test performance could make the meaning of a particular IQ score drift over time.

This is one reason an IQ score should always be interpreted in the context of the test and norms used to produce it.

Is the Flynn effect still happening?

The trend has not continued at the same rate everywhere. Research in several countries has found that earlier increases in test performance slowed or levelled off, and some studies have reported declines during later periods.

When average performance decreases after an earlier period of increases, researchers sometimes refer to this as a reverse Flynn effect.

However, these later patterns are not identical across countries, tests or populations. The timing and size of changes can vary, so the Flynn effect should not be treated as a simple worldwide increase or decrease that applies equally to everyone.

Why IQ scores from different generations are difficult to compare

An IQ score is a standardized number, not a raw count of correct answers. Its meaning depends partly on the reference population and scoring system used by the test.

For that reason, comparing an IQ score from an older test directly with a modern IQ score can be misleading if the different norms, test versions and testing conditions are not considered.

Does the Flynn effect affect IQ percentiles?

IQ percentiles are also connected to the reference distribution used by a test. Because modern IQ scales are standardized against their norm groups, the same numerical IQ score is interpreted relative to the distribution established for that scale.

What the Flynn effect teaches us about IQ

The Flynn effect is important because it shows that performance on intelligence tests can change across generations. It provides evidence that cognitive test performance is influenced by environmental, educational, health and historical factors.

At the same time, it does not mean that IQ scores are meaningless. Standardized tests can still provide useful information when they are properly designed, normed and interpreted within their intended context.

The key point is that an IQ score is a standardized measurement whose interpretation depends on the test, norms and population behind it.

Flynn effect in simple terms

Imagine that people from two generations take different versions of an intelligence test. The later generation may perform better on the underlying tasks. When the newer test is normed, however, its scoring system is adjusted so that the new population still has an average IQ of about 100.

So the Flynn effect is about changes in test performance across generations, while the IQ scale is repeatedly adjusted through re-norming.

One important distinction

A rise in average IQ-test performance does not automatically mean that people became universally smarter. The Flynn effect describes a historical pattern in performance on particular cognitive tests, and different abilities can show different trends.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Flynn effect?

The Flynn effect is the observed rise in average performance on many intelligence tests across generations, particularly during much of the 20th century.

Who discovered the Flynn effect?

The effect is named after James Flynn, a New Zealand researcher who documented and studied generational changes in intelligence test performance.

Why is the Flynn effect important?

It shows that performance on intelligence tests can change across generations. This is important when interpreting IQ scores and comparing results across different historical periods.

Why does the average IQ stay at 100?

IQ tests are periodically re-normed using newer reference populations. The scoring system is designed so that the average score of the new norm group remains around 100.

Is there a reverse Flynn effect?

Some research has reported periods of declining average test performance after earlier gains. This pattern is sometimes called the reverse Flynn effect, although its size and timing vary across populations and tests.

Does the Flynn effect mean IQ is increasing forever?

No. The pattern has varied over time and between populations. Some later studies have found slower gains, stable performance or declines rather than a continuing increase.
