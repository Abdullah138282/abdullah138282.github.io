How the IQ percentile calculator works

IQ scores are built to follow a normal distribution, the familiar bell curve. The average is set to 100, and the standard deviation (the typical spread around the average) is 15 on most modern tests. The calculator turns your score into a z-score, which counts how many standard deviations you are from 100, and then reads the percentile from the normal curve.

For example, a score of 115 is one standard deviation above average. That puts you at about the 84th percentile: higher than roughly 84 out of 100 people.

How to read your result

A percentile describes rank, not ability in absolute terms. Two people with IQs of 98 and 102 are almost indistinguishable, even though their percentiles differ by a few points. Differences matter most at a distance: the gap between 85 and 115 is meaningful, the gap between 100 and 103 is not.

Every IQ test also has a margin of error, usually several points even for professionally administered tests. Free online tests, including ours, have a wider one. Use the number as a rough guide and read the IQ score guide for what each range means. If you have not tested yet, you can try the Thinkora IQ test.

Questions about IQ percentiles

What does an IQ percentile mean?

A percentile shows the share of people who score below you. If your IQ is at the 90th percentile, you scored higher than about 90 out of every 100 people in the comparison group.

What percentile is an IQ of 130?

On the standard scale (mean 100, standard deviation 15), an IQ of 130 is at about the 97.7th percentile. Roughly 1 in 44 people score 130 or higher.

What IQ is the 50th percentile?

An IQ of 100. Tests are designed so that 100 is the median and the mean of the comparison group.

Why do different tests give different percentiles for the same score?

Some tests use a different standard deviation. Most modern tests use 15, some older Stanford–Binet editions used 16, and the Cattell culture-fair scales use 24. The same number means a different rank on each scale, so pick the standard deviation your test uses.

Can I use this to check my result from an online test?

Yes, for a rough comparison. Online tests are not normed against a large, representative sample, so treat the percentile as an estimate, not a diagnosis.
